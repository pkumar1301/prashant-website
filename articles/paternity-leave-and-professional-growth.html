How Paternity Leave Taught Me Valuable Lessons For A Better Professional

Prashant Kumar · December 28, 2020

The year 2020 will go down in history as the year which changed the world. From the COVID-19 pandemic to Black Lives Matter protests, the bushfires across Australia/US or the stock market crash, calling this year eventful would be an understatement.

However, for me personally, this year has brought unimaginable joy when I became father to a little girl in May. I have spent every single day watching her grow and even after all this time, I still find myself wondering if this is real. Working From Home (WFH) has been an incredible help in allowing me to support my wife in childcare. But when the time came for her to return to work and for me to take my paternity leave, in all honesty, I feared how I will do it. I was wracked by the same questions of whether I can even manage it all alone or because I am taking 3 months leave (thanks to a generous Spanish Paternity leave policy), it will impact my yearly performance review and career progression.

I will be left behind in this competitive world and will lose the opportunity to advance.

As I near the end of my Paternity Leave, I can whole heartedly confirm that it has made me into a better professional and a much better human being. I am writing this article for all those would-be fathers who might be asking the same questions as me, and my advice to them is that take this time off. It is the best time investment you can make for your family and yourself.

According to a research by Harvard Business School, more male employees (35%) than female employees (23%) felt care responsibilities hurt their career. More men (40%) than women (25%) also strongly agreed that "caregivers are perceived to be less committed to their careers than non-caregivers".

Some of the most common reasons given for men not taking paternity leave (even when it is available and paid for) are:

  • The traditional gender roles mindset that it is a mother's job to take care of the kids while a father must go out, work hard and support the family
  • The attitude of bosses and companies towards paternity leave can be antiquated and conservative
  • The fear of facing unreasonable workload when they return to work ("I Hope you are rested and refreshed after your break as we have an intense time ahead")
  • The attached stigma that paternity leave will damage the professional reputation of a man along with his future earning potential as he is no longer seen as committed to work

When I started the paternity, I was unsure of how will I pass time? Going from 10-hour workdays, managing multiple meetings and stakeholders to taking care of 5 months old baby, I was convinced that I would be bored out of my head within a few days. But I was proven wrong (and some), and boy do I have new sense of respect for my mother who was a full-time mom taking care of 2 growing boys. In this journey, I discovered that childcare helped me develop my soft skills and organizational skills which have a direct impact on my day-to-day work. Sharing my learnings from experience:

Patience: It is 2 AM and my daughter has decided that it is the perfect time to practice sitting up and checking the texture of her daddy's beard. I had to spend the next 2 hours with her in the living room watching her play with her toys and waiting for her to feel sleepy again. Ask any parent, there is no way you can hurry a baby in falling sleep again. It is a test of your patience, to the limit. What I learnt here that instead of sitting there disgruntled and cranky, why not play with her and interact more (It ended up with us dancing to Disney songs and her mom waking up due to the noise). I cannot speed it up, may as well enjoy it. So next time at work, If I have to wait patiently for my dev team to finish a delayed project or a colleague to complete and analysis, instead of being disgruntled, I will channel this experience and enjoy the extra time.

Persistence: 6 months have passed and its time to introduce solid foods to my daughters. That translates to spending 40-minutes of effort to make her eat 5 tablespoons of puree. Half of which will end up on the floor for my dog to lick off. But there is no way around this. I must persist through and feed her. When I am back to office, that persistence training will help me push through bottlenecks because sometimes there are no shortcuts to work. You just have to keep trying till it gets completed.

Focus: Every parent will testify to this, when you are with a small baby, you have to be with the baby. You cannot zone out, check your phone or emails. Trust me, I have tried, and my daughter has dutifully reminded me to give attention to her. And watching her play with the same set of toys for 60 minutes plus has trained me well for the big group meetings we all have to take while working.

Handling Crisis: I still remember the first week when we were back from the hospital. She would cry all the time. I had not imagined how stressful a crying baby can be, simply because it hurts you at an emotional level. I have handled crisis at work, but this was another level. And after going through that week, I am now confident that I can handle anything work can throw at me.

Emotional Intelligence: Babies can sense your mood better than you yourself can understand sometimes. So, no matter how annoyed, angry or frustrated I get, I must control those emotions so that it doesn't affect my baby's mood. When she is crying or being fussy, I have learnt to take a deep breath, put a smile on my face and take care of her with a positive loving attitude. Me getting worked up will only worsen the situation. Now, take the same thought process and apply it to an office situation. How much better will be a team situation if you can handle your emotions during a tight deadline or a tensed negotiation. Human beings communicate 70% of their thoughts non-verbally. Emotional intelligence is a key factor in professional success

Leadership by Example: I see the way my daughter looks me. She observes my every action, listens to every word. And she is learning how to interact with this world through me. So, I have to be the best example for her. Similarly, when you are leading a team or mentoring someone, they learn from your behaviors and actions. Your colleagues observe you and you can lead them through your actions and choices. The positive life lessons you are teaching to your baby, you can embody those same lessons at work and become the leader.

Time for yourself: The day-to-day work life is very hectic. We get caught up in grind and we forget to take the time off for ourselves. To contemplate our choices, plan our future and decide what we really want in life and career. My paternity gave me the chance to take a step back and evaluate my career journey so far and where I want to go from here. It allowed me to spend time with myself, to think and plan my future, to invest in myself. I cannot stress the importance of investing in yourself enough. Use the paternity to take time for yourself and take a step back and evaluate.

Putting aside all the things I learnt while taking care of my daughter, I would repeat the whole experience again in heartbeat, just to experience the joy of watching her grow every single day. Work, career, advancement - all these things will always be there. But the precious moments of a growing baby will never come back. So take that paternity leave and enjoy it to the fullest.
